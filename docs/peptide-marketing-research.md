Prepared by NuVue for Perspective Health Iowa, 2026-10-16. This is marketing research, not legal advice. Have a healthcare attorney review the page before it goes live.

## The short version for Jess

### Do

- **Do** sell the *care*, not the *molecule*. Talk about the evaluation, the labs, Stacey's oversight, the follow-ups, and the convenience of virtual visits.
- **Do** use hedged wellness language: "may support," "as part of a personalized plan," "prescribed only when clinically appropriate," "results vary."
- **Do** include this disclosure anywhere compounded peptides come up: *"Compounded medications are prepared by a licensed pharmacy for individual patients and are not FDA-approved."*
- **Do** say plainly that patients must be **physically located in Iowa** during each virtual visit.
- **Do** get telehealth consent and review the patient's records before the first visit. Iowa requires both.
- **Do** name the compounding pharmacy if asked, and in the patient paperwork. FDA warning letters have hit telehealth brands that hid who actually compounds the drug.
- **Do** consider listing Stacey as **"Stacey Darnold, ARNP, FNP-C."** ARNP is Iowa's licensed title.
- **Do** answer "Is BPC-157 legal?"-type questions factually in blog or FAQ content. Educating people is far lower risk than offering the product.

### Don't

- **Don't** name or price these on the website, in ads, on GBP, or on social media for now: BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, AOD-9604, injectable GHK-Cu, Melanotan II, KPV, MOTs-C, selank, semax, epitalon, DSIP, LL-37, or thymosin alpha-1. Here's why:
  - None of them can lawfully be compounded by a 503A pharmacy today.
  - FDA is actively sending warning letters about them.
  - Naming them would block Google and Meta ad approval and LegitScript certification.
- **Don't** call a compounded peptide "FDA-approved," "generic," or "the same as" a brand drug. That exact wording drove 100+ FDA warning letters in 2025-2026.
- **Don't** say a peptide treats, cures, heals, reverses, or prevents anything. Also avoid "clinically proven" and even "clinically tested."
- **Don't** use before/after photos or patient results as proof that a peptide works. Testimonials don't count as scientific evidence under FTC rules, and a disclaimer doesn't fix a misleading claim.
- **Don't** source anything labeled "research use only" or "not for human consumption." FDA says that label is no defense.
- **Don't** run paid Google or Meta ads for peptide prescribing until the clinic holds **LegitScript certification**. GLP-1s count too.

### Names that are OK to use, with care

- **Sermorelin.** It's the most common peptide that can lawfully be compounded. Name it only with the compounding disclosure and no outcome claims. Note that its exact FDA basis isn't confirmed from a primary source: it was part of a previously approved drug that was withdrawn for commercial reasons.
- **FDA-approved brand drugs**, only if the brand product is what's actually dispensed. Examples: semaglutide (Wegovy/Ozempic), tirzepatide (Zepbound/Mounjaro), tesamorelin (Egrifta), bremelanotide/PT-141 (Vyleesi).

**What the draft page does:** it names **no** specific peptides. That keeps it safe whichever way the HHS policy lands. It also leaves room to add sermorelin, or anything newly allowed, later.

## 1. FDA status of common peptides

**How a 503A pharmacy can compound legally.** The bulk ingredient must meet one of three conditions:

- it has a USP monograph,
- it is a component of an FDA-approved drug, or
- it is on FDA's 503A "bulks list."

Source: [FDA 503A page](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/bulk-drug-substances-used-compounding-under-section-503a-fdc-act).

| Peptide | Status today | Safe to name? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Semaglutide, tirzepatide | FDA-approved brand drugs. Compounded "copies" have been generally barred since the 2025 shortage ended (503A enforcement discretion ended Feb 18 and Apr 22, 2025). | Brand only |
| Tesamorelin (Egrifta), bremelanotide/PT-141 (Vyleesi) | FDA-approved brand drugs. Compounded tesamorelin sellers got FDA letters in Aug-Sep 2026. | Brand only |
| Sermorelin | Compounded as a component of a previously approved drug (Geref, withdrawn in 2008 for commercial reasons). | Yes, with disclosure |
| BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, AOD-9604, GHK-Cu (injectable), Melanotan II, KPV, MOTs-C, selank, semax, epitalon, DSIP, LL-37, thymosin alpha-1 | Removed from FDA's Category 2 ("significant safety risk") list in April 2026 because the nominators withdrew them. **That does not make them legal to compound.** They are not on the bulks list and have no enforcement discretion, so they sit in a "regulatory grey area." | No |
| Ipamorelin | Withdrawn nomination for 503A, but **still Category 2 for 503B** outsourcing facilities. | No |
| Kisspeptin-10, ibutamoren, GHRP-2/-6 | Still in Category 2. | No |

Sources: [FDA Category 2 page, current as of 4/22/26](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/certain-bulk-drug-substances-use-compounding-may-present-significant-safety-risks) (verified 10/16); [Polsinelli, 4/22/26](https://www.polsinelli.com/publications/what-fdas-latest-actions-mean-for-peptide-compounding); [DJ Holt Law, Apr 2026](https://djholtlaw.com/regulatory-alert-fdas-april-2026-update-on-bpc-157-and-ghk-cu/).

**FDA advisory committee (PCAC), July 23-24, 2026**

- The committee voted to recommend adding BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTs-C, epitalon, and semax to the bulks list. It voted against DSIP.
- FDA's own reviewers recommended against all seven.
- The votes are not binding. Adding a peptide to the bulks list requires rulemaking, which can take years.
- Five more peptides go to the committee by Feb 2027.

Sources: [STAT, 7/23/26](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/23/fda-panel-okays-peptides-compound-pharmacies-bpc-157-kpv/); [Mintz, 7/29/26](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2146/2026-07-29-fdas-advisory-committee-votes-peptides-what-it-does-and).

**FDA enforcement trend.** The letters show what FDA is watching:

- **Sept 2025:** 100+ warning letters, Hims among them, for "same as Ozempic" claims ([HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/drug-center/fda-cracks-down-on-hims-other-telehealth-companies-over-drug-ads)).
- **Mar 2026:** 30 telehealth letters, partly for not disclosing who compounds the drug ([Pharmaceutical Commerce, 3/4/26](https://www.pharmaceuticalcommerce.com/view/fda-issues-30-warning-letters-to-telehealth-firms-over-misleading-compounded-glp-1-marketing)).
- **Jun 2026:** 25 more telehealth letters.
- **Aug 2026:** letters to online peptide vendors. FDA said "Research Use Only" labels are no defense.
- **Aug-Sep 2026:** letters to sellers of compounded ipamorelin and tesamorelin.

Digest: [JD Supra](https://jdsupra.com/topics/drug-compounding/enforcement-actions/).

## 2. Ad platform rules

**Google Ads**

- Ads for prescription drugs or telemedicine require Google certification, and telemedicine providers must be **LegitScript-certified** ([Google Healthcare & medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)).
- Google's unapproved-substances list doesn't name BPC-157 or other peptides ([list](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/2423645)).
- Its catch-all rules do ban products under regulatory warning, and non-approved products marketed as safe or effective. Expect peptide-name ads to be disapproved. That last point is our inference, not Google's stated position.

**Meta (Facebook/Instagram)**

- Prescription drug ads require active LegitScript certification plus Meta authorization.
- They must target ages 18+ and include a "consult a licensed professional" style disclaimer ([Meta Drugs & Pharmaceuticals](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drugs-pharmaceuticals/)).
- General ads for the clinic's telehealth *service* and educational ads don't need written authorization.
- Weight-related ads must target 18+, can't use before/after images, and can't play on negative self-image ([Meta Health & Wellness](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness)).
- Avoid copy that implies the viewer has a condition, such as "Struggling with low energy?" This is long-standing Meta practice, but we couldn't load the current policy page to confirm it.

**LegitScript Healthcare Certification**

- Cost: $975 application fee plus $2,150 per year, or an extra $2,500 for expedited review ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/)).
- A business that offers unapproved peptides is unlikely to pass. LegitScript specifically flagged BPC-157, TB-500, AOD-9604, and Melanotan listings in Dec 2025 ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251209572056/en)).
- **Recommendation:** grow peptides organically first (SEO, GBP, current patients, email). Revisit paid ads once Jess decides whether LegitScript is worth roughly $3k in year one.

## 3. Iowa rules for Stacey's telehealth prescribing

- **Independent practice.** Iowa ARNPs practice independently, with no collaborative agreement required ([Iowa DIAL](https://dial.iowa.gov/licenses/medical/nursing-professional-midwifery/nursing-practice/arnp-role-and-scope)). The rules now sit in **481 IAC ch. 621** (moved 6/11/25).
- **Telehealth rule 481-621.9**, effective 6/4/25 ([IAC PDF](https://www.legis.iowa.gov/docs/iac/chapter/09-30-2026.481.621.pdf)):
  - The patient must be **physically in Iowa** for Stacey's Iowa license to cover the visit.
  - Care must meet the same standard as an in-person visit.
  - Stacey must review the patient's records before the visit.
  - The provider-patient relationship *can* be established over telehealth.
  - Written telehealth consent is required.
  - The video platform must be HIPAA-compliant.
  - Telehealth prescriptions are allowed when they match the in-person standard.
- **Controlled substances.** The peptides discussed here are not DEA-scheduled. This comes from a commercial source, not a DEA document. Testosterone *is* Schedule III. DEA's telemedicine flexibilities for controlled substances run through **Dec 31, 2026** ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/dea-telemedicine-flexibilities-for-controlled-substances-2026/)).
- **Iowa enforcement.** We found no Iowa Board of Pharmacy peptide guidance and no Iowa AG action against med spas or peptide clinics. Other states are active, though:
  - Ohio's AG warned 14 med spas in 2025 ([Spectrum](https://spectrumnews1.com/oh/columbus/news/2025/04/16/attorney-general-sends-warnings-to-med-spas-for-weight-loss-drugs)).
  - Alabama's AG closed an IV clinic in Nov 2025 ([AmSpa](https://www.americanmedspa.org/news/alabama-attorney-general-closes-med-spa-for-providing-unapproved-weight-loss-drugs-lawsuit-filed/)).

## 4. Claim limits (FTC and FDA)

- **FTC standard.** Health claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence," which generally means randomized human trials. Testimonials aren't evidence, and disclaimers don't cure a deceptive claim ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)).
- **FDA misbranding.** Claiming a compounded drug is FDA-approved, or the same as a brand drug, is misbranding.

| Safer | Off-limits |
| --- | --- |
| "may support energy and overall well-being as part of a care plan" | "boosts energy," "builds muscle," "heals injuries" |
| "prescribed only after an evaluation, when clinically appropriate" | "BPC-157 for joint pain," "TB-500 recovery protocol" |
| "compounded medications are not FDA-approved" | "FDA-approved peptides" (for compounded products) |
| "results vary" | "clinically proven," before/after photos as proof |
| "virtual visits for patients located in Iowa" | "available nationwide" |

**Already fixed in the draft:** the old Supplementary Services blurb said peptides "can boost energy, improve muscle tone." It now reads "may support energy and overall well-being as part of a comprehensive health plan" and links to the new page.
